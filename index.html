Why there are no dollar figures

Local rates are stable and quotable; one-way rates are dynamically priced by lane, season, and fleet imbalance, and can swing several-fold between two quotes a week apart. Printing dollar amounts would make this reference confidently wrong about the number you care most about. So every record describes the rate structure — what you're charged for, and what gets charged again on top — and leaves the amount to the live quote.

What the $ tiers mean

Ordinal, not absolute. A 26-foot truck will still cost more than a 4×8 trailer in ten years, so relative rank survives inflation and lane pricing in a way that dollar figures don't. Each record carries two, because the whole point of this reference is that local and one-way pricing behave differently:

  • Local — total out the door for one day and roughly 50 miles, including the per-mile charge and fuel.
  • One-way — total out the door on a lane of roughly 1,000 miles, including fuel and any mileage overage.
  • n/a — the vendor doesn't offer that trip type. On the one-way column this is the single most decision-relevant field in the index: everything Enterprise, everything Home Depot, every equipment yard, and every U-Haul utility trailer is local-only.

The tiers diverge in ways worth reading for. U-Haul's 6×12 cargo trailer is $$$$ locally but $$$$ one-way, because trailers carry no per-mile charge and the advantage compounds with distance. Penske climbs a tier on long lanes despite unlimited miles, because its day rate starts higher. Home Depot is $$$$ for 75 minutes and would be off the top of the scale for a full day.

These are my judgment calls, not quotes. They're anchored to the two scenarios above so you can audit them — if a tier looks wrong against a real quote you pulled, it probably is, and the note field on each record is the place to say so.

How to read the spec plate

  • GVWR — gross vehicle weight rating. The whole rig loaded. Crossing 26,001 lbs is what triggers CDL territory, which is why every consumer truck stops at 25,999.
  • MAX LOAD — payload. Cargo only, not counting you, passengers, or fuel. Almost nobody hits it; volume runs out first.
  • EMPTY — tare weight. On trailers, this is what your hitch is pulling before anything goes in.
  • TONGUE — downward force on the ball. Loading a trailer back-heavy to reduce it causes sway, which is the failure mode that puts trailers in ditches.

Confidence flags

Every record carries one. High means specs are stable and widely published. Med means the figure is right in shape but may be a model year off. Low means verify before relying on it — trailer interior volumes are the worst offenders, since published cubic-foot claims frequently don't reconcile with the published interior dimensions.

What this covers

Consumer moving only. No CDL freight, no equipment haulers beyond what a rental yard will hand a walk-up customer, no RV or travel trailers.

Where the vendors actually differ

Not on trucks — a 16-foot box is a 16-foot box. They differ on structure, and the structure is what decides the bill:

  • U-Haul — the only national one-way trailer network, and the only company that will rent you a towable to pull behind your own car. Meters miles on one-way.
  • Penske — unlimited miles on one-way. The advantage compounds with distance and is invisible on the quote page. Far fewer locations.
  • Budget — lowest sticker, oldest fleet, metered one-way miles, and a discount structure so aggressive that paying the headline rate is a mistake. No 22-foot option, so a mid-size load sizes up to a 26.
  • Enterprise — newest fleet, liftgates nobody else offers, and no one-way. Structurally a local-work vendor. Its heavy pickups are the best rental tow vehicle available anywhere.
  • Home Depot — priced by a 75-minute clock with unlimited miles. Unbeatable for the trip home from the store, worst per-hour rate here for anything longer.
  • Equipment yards — heavier gear, weekly rate tiers, and no hand-holding. They will not check whether your truck can pull what they hand you.

The towing rules nobody reads first

These apply to every U-Haul towable and are the reason rentals get refused at the counter. The binding rule is not hitch class — it's the last one:

  • Hitch ball must be 1-7/8" or 2", rated to at least 2,000 lbs, mounted no higher than 25" off the ground.
  • External mirrors on both sides of the tow vehicle.
  • SUVs and select Jeeps must have a hard top.
  • Trailer and tow vehicle lighting operational at all times, day or night.
  • Maximum recommended towing speed is 55 mph. This is not advisory garnish — trailer sway is speed-dependent.
  • The tow vehicle's curb weight must exceed the weight of the loaded trailer. This catches more people than any hitch specification. Empty U-Haul trailers alone run from 630 to 3,155 lbs, and their online quote system computes your allowance from your vehicle's curb weight, hitch rating, and the trailer's empty weight before it will take the reservation.
  • Load roughly 60% of the weight ahead of the axle. Back-heavy is how trailers sway, and sway at speed is the failure mode that ends badly.

The volume calculator

Cubic-foot figures in the inventory counter are packed volumes — they include the dead air around awkward shapes, since a sofa does not tessellate. Add roughly 10% if you pack loosely. Boxes stack to the ceiling and furniture does not, so a load that is mostly boxes will beat its estimate and a load that is mostly furniture will not.

The curb weight rule

The picker checks your tow vehicle's curb weight against the fully loaded trailer, because that is U-Haul's actual requirement and it is stricter than the hitch rating on most vehicles. A 6×12 cargo trailer is 1,920 lbs empty and carries 2,480 lbs, so 4,400 lbs loaded — which your vehicle must outweigh, not merely be rated to pull. Plenty of SUVs rated for 5,000 lbs of towing weigh less than 4,400 and are therefore ineligible.

Colour coding

Each vehicle type carries a colour drawn from the MUTCD highway sign palette — guide green for box trucks, services blue for vans, recreational brown for pickups, and so on through the towables. It runs along the card edge, the ID chip, and the type filter chips, so you can see which band of the index you are in without reading a word. A scanning aid, not decoration.
